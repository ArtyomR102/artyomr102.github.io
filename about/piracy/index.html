How it all started

Billions of years ago, Earth was a giant ball of hot gas… A bit too far back, huh?

Well, to save time, let's just say that back then, nobody suspected where all this was heading. And it was heading toward the emergence of relatively intelligent beings – humans. Since all the skill points went into intelligence, a single human can't survive on their own. They constantly need to exchange stuff with other humans – food, clothes, tools. Of course, nobody wants to give things away for free, so first came barter, then someone came up with using gold as a universal value equivalent, then money appeared, currency, and so on. For centuries, this system worked perfectly – because in our world, you can't just copy a physical object. But then came the late 20th century. Computers reached a somewhat acceptable speed, and people started trying to make money with them.

Turning point

At first, all software was distributed on physical media – floppy disks, cassettes, even magnetic tape reels. There was barely any permanent storage in computers, so not many people could simply copy a program to their own machine. But over time, people figured out that cassettes could be copied using a tape recorder, most PCs had two floppy drives, and blank disks became cheap and available. In other words, nothing stopped you from taking a floppy with software and copying it sector by sector onto another floppy, ending up with two floppies – which could then be copied again.

Remember what I said about copying a physical object? This single fact breaks classic economics right away – why would I spend money on something if I can just borrow it from someone, copy it and give it back? Of course, programmers' income started dropping very fast, and they had to do something about it.

The Copy Wars

Since then, the world has split into two major factions – those who wanted to protect the rights of creators (let's call them the "paladins") and those who loved getting stuff for free (let's call them the "copycats"). Hostilities immediately broke out on several fronts:

The Software Front

Unsurprisingly, the paladins had a lot of programmers among them, so they started coming up with all sorts of ways to make life harder for the copycats. They invented various protection methods: from requiring a code from the box and checking for disc in the drive, all the way to complex multi-layered DRM systems that required online verification and ate up all system resources.

Meanwhile, the copycats had no shortage of hackers. They had already been successfully creating disk copying programs, and now they started inventing countermeasures – cracking already-protected games, creating keygens and server emulators to trick DRMs, using torrents for distributed file sharing and so on.

The Legal Front

After a while, the paladins realized the software battle would last forever, so they took a different approach. They started pushing their main idea – copying software is nothing but theft, and should be punishable accordingly. That's how we got DMCA, EUCD, MPAA, RIAA and other acronyms that set the copycats' rum mugs shaking. The copycats can't really do anything, 'cause their lobbying power is far smaller, even though they themselves are far more numerous. All that's left is to poke holes in the paladins' laws based on questionable edge cases and slowly fade into the shadows.

The Moral Front

The most interesting – and the dumbest – of them all. It all started when the paladins called the copycats thieves. The copycats weren't having any of it and immediately pointed out the logical flaw: classical economics doesn't apply to digital goods, so calling it "theft" is a huge stretch. The paladins fired back that copying reduces the income of content creators, which technically makes it equivalent to stealing money. The copycats countered that without copying, many games – like NES titles – would have remained completely unknown.

When both sides ran out of constructive arguments and the paladins had effectively won on the legal front, the copycats started grasping at all sorts of facts, just to have something to say. First, they nitpicked about DRM – how they dig into the system, behave like malware and fight against legitimate buyers (hello, StarForce). Of course, this argument kinda backfired. Then they remembered that many modern games are live-service games tied to servers – that's how the famous phrase was born: If buying isn't owning, then pirating isn't stealing.

And let's not pretend the paladins are saints. By this point, many of them had learned to wield the same principles – not for justice, but for profit. Extra profit, the kind they wouldn't have gotten even if piracy never existed. The MP3 scene got hit the hardest – even today, with piracy nearly a mortal sin in many countries, the volume of this argument hasn't dropped a single decibel.

Prince from the Dark

So who's right in the end? Um, seriously, that's your question? Ok then, probably neither. But don't tell them that – they'd have nothing left to fight about. And honestly? I think they just enjoy it, why ruin all the fun?

…except there's another fraction of humanity that's always left in the shadow… Not because they're weak or something, but because they aren't trying to be loud. They just do their thing and label it with a CC logo.

Of course, I'm talking about open licenses!

Seriously, these guys made so many things! Linux powers the absolute majority of devices, from servers to phones to fridges. Kubbi, Kevin MacLeod and other composers make music you can use freely in videos without fear of copyright claims. Wikipedia runs on open content. Blender, GIMP, Audacity – all free, all legal, all awesome! What are you waiting for? Go make something, then gift it!

And here's the kicker: nobody had to steal anything! Nobody had to sue anyone! Someone just wrote "You can use this, here's how" – and the world said "Cool, thanks!" Why are you still reading this? Come and make something, then share it with the world! USE SOMETHING! FORK SOMETHING! RIGHT NOW!! WHAT ARE YOU WAITING FOR?!

And remember kids – CC is always your friend!

Okay, okay, calm down! 😅 Before you think I'm braindead, it was obviously a joke. Yes, open licenses are great, yes, they solved the problem for a lot of people. But let's be real – they're not for everyone either. Try telling a AAA game publisher: "Hey, just release Call of Duty under CC BY-SA". Open licenses work wonders for code, music, art, knowledge. But for blockbuster entertainment? The one where investors expect millions in return? Yeah... not so much.

So the war continues. Paladins still lock things down. Copycats still crack them open. And the open license folks just sit in the middle, sipping tea, saying "told you so". No winners. No losers. Just the Internet being the Internet.
